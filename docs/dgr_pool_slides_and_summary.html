Slide 1 — Where the $1.5bn ancillary-fund pool can flow after DGR reform

Story. PAFs and PuAFs distributed $1,527m in 2023-24 (PAF $914m / PuAF $613m; net assets $17.5bn and compounding). This money can only go to DGR Item 1 charities — so DGR reform redraws the map of who can receive it. Under both scenarios ~17,900 charities become newly eligible; under PC pure change ~2,900 charities also lose access. Ancillary funds themselves keep their DGR under both scenarios (the PC retains “giving funds”; its removals target recipient activity classes), so the pool stays intact.

Method. Fixed-pool redistribution bounds. Charity-level eligibility flags from our reform mapping (latest AIS-year, ancillary funds excluded from the recipient base); pool split between incumbents and gainers under three mechanical allocation rules — pro-rata to donations, to revenue, or equal per charity. The ATO never publishes recipient-level flows, so these bound access, not predicted grants.

Findings (visual: the new two-panel hero chart in the notebook)

  • Gainers could access $109m–$684m/yr (7% of the pool under the donations rule → 45% under equal-per-charity). PC is marginally more redistributive than Expansion because losers exit the base.
  • PC pure change also displaces ~$150–180m/yr (≈11% of today's pool) currently accessible to the charities that lose DGR.
  • Recipients' reported donations cap plausibility: the donations rule pins every group at 16.7% of what it actually raises; count-rule figures for small cohorts are upper bounds.

So what. Expansion grows access without disrupting anyone; PC pure change moves more money but creates a losing side worth ~$170m/yr of pool access. Either way, meaningful new philanthropic capital opens up to newly-endorsed charities — if they can actually attract it, which is Slide 2.

Slide 2 — Will newly-endorsed charities capture it? Evidence from past “switchers”

Story. We tested whether DGR endorsement actually changes giving, using 646 established charities that gained DGR during 2019–24 as a pseudo-counterfactual experiment.

Method. Event study around each charity's endorsement year (ABR day-level endorsement dates mapped to financial years), CPI-adjusted, with two comparison cohorts (always-DGR, never-DGR), a placebo test on random fake endorsement dates, and a baseline-dependence split.

Findings (visual: event-time step vs placebo chart — alt1_event_placebo.png)

  • Real (CPI-adjusted) donations to switchers rise ~+30% within-charity after endorsement — a clear step at year 0, absent in the placebo.
  • Median real donation growth 2019→2024: switchers +38% vs 0% for always-DGR and 0% for never-DGR — the effect is not sector trend.
  • It's a one-off level shift, not a new growth trajectory; charities with low donation-dependence gain most (share of income from donations ~2%→9%).

So what. Endorsement genuinely unlocks giving. The gainer ranges on Slide 1 aren't just theoretical access — history says newly-endorsed charities convert endorsement into real donation growth, supporting the case that reform redirects philanthropic dollars toward the newly eligible.

Summary of the last few days' work

  1. Checked the teammate's “donation share before/after DGR” analysis, then rebuilt it properly. Mapped ABR day-level endorsement dates to financial years (respecting each charity's FY end month), built a 2019–2024 panel (we extended AIS ingestion back to 2019), and gated to 646 established switchers to avoid new-charity startup artefacts.

  2. Key methodological pivot — from “share of revenue” to median dollar changes. The original framing (donations as % of revenue) is flat (~0.30–0.33) before and after endorsement — but that's because total income grows in step with donations, not because endorsement does nothing. Switching to within-charity median real donation levels revealed the true effect: a ~+30% one-off step at endorsement.

  3. Pseudo-counterfactual testing to establish the step is causal-ish, not artefact:

    • Three-cohort comparison: switchers +37.8% median real growth 2019→2024 vs 0% for always-DGR and 0% for never-DGR.
    • Placebo test: random fake endorsement years show no step (seed-controlled).
    • Anticipation check: modest pre-endorsement drift only; dip at t−2 diagnosed as a 2023-cohort COVID/composition artefact, not behaviour.
    • Baseline-dependence split: low-dependence charities gain most; high-dependence show regression to the mean.

  4. Reform scenario modelling from the team's eligibility mapping (PC pure change vs Expansion): charity-level counts (23.5k keepers, 17.9k gainers, 2.9k ordinary losers), donor-side uplift estimates ($172–337m/yr at 24–47% uplift), and the pool redistribution above.
