Unlock DGR Analysis — Project Summary

Justice Connect Unlock DGR campaign · 2026 Actuaries Institute Hackathon · 10 July 2026

The aim

Justice Connect's Unlock DGR campaign argues that Australia's Deductible Gift Recipient (DGR) framework arbitrarily excludes thousands of charities — you can claim a tax deduction for donating to some causes but not others, based on categories largely frozen since the 1990s. For the 2026 Actuaries Institute Hackathon, our task is to build a rigorous, public-data evidence base quantifying who benefits, and by how much, if DGR eligibility expands to four campaign priority cohorts: neighbourhood houses, disaster preparedness, injury prevention, and human rights charities.

What we built

A fully reproducible R {targets} pipeline that downloads, cleans, links, and validates every relevant public dataset:

  • ACNC Charity Register (65,430 charities) — the foundation; everything joins to it by ABN.
  • ABR API DGR lookups — individual endorsement queries for all 65,430 ABNs (no bulk list exists): 27,623 DGR-endorsed (42%), 37,807 not (58%).
  • ACNC Annual Information Statements 2021–2024 — harmonised into a 211,895-row charity-year financials panel via an explicit column mapping that errors loudly on schema drift.
  • AIS program-level CLASSIE classifications — what charities actually do, which proved decisive for cohort identification.
  • ATO Taxation Statistics — 44 years of individual giving ($9.1B claimed in 2022-23), donor demographics, DGR counts by category, and PAF/PuAF ancillary fund series to 2023-24.

Plus a 22-check validation report against published anchors, refreshed documentation (data dictionary from real schemas, pipeline map, plain-language stakeholder brief, volunteer DuckDB setup), all committed and pushed.

Chain of thought for the overall plan

The reasoning chain that shaped the design:

  1. The naive question is unanswerable. "How much money would flow to newly eligible charities?" cannot be answered directly — the ATO publishes no recipient-level data on where PAF/PuAF distributions go, and no dataset tracks charities before/after DGR endorsement. Chasing this would have wasted the hackathon.
  2. Reframe as access, not flows. Ancillary funds (PAFs/PuAFs) are legally required to distribute only to DGRs. Their $1.53B/yr in distributions (FY2023-24) is a structurally locked pool. Reform doesn't guarantee money moves — but it removes a legal barrier. So model potential access to the pool, presented honestly as scenario ranges, not point predictions.
  3. Split benefit into two distinct layers so effects aren't double-counted and each gets the right method:
    • Layer 1 — Redistribution: existing philanthropic dollars becoming accessible (the ancillary pool).
    • Layer 2 — Pie growth: DGR endorsement increases total giving (the Productivity Commission's $1.50-per-$1 multiplier). We cite the PC figure rather than pretend to re-derive it, but build a cross-sectional plausibility check: the donations gap between DGR and non-DGR charities within size × sector strata.
  4. "Who benefits" blocks everything. No estimate is credible without defensible cohort definitions. The original purpose+name-keyword approach was too conservative (e.g. only 79 disaster preparedness charities). Program-level CLASSIE data widened this dramatically — but every expansion rule was made auditable: a rules file where only human-whitelisted rules count, candidates reported separately for review. Final frozen cohorts: neighbourhood houses 312, disaster preparedness 387, injury prevention 237, human rights 851 — 1,745 distinct charities.
  5. Measure leakage. A large share of each cohort already has DGR (66.7% of disaster preparedness, 62.7% human rights, 67.1% injury prevention — but only 25% of neighbourhood houses). Only the newly eligible members can benefit, so every estimate is leakage-adjusted. This is also a campaign insight in itself: neighbourhood houses are the most excluded cohort.
  6. Be honest about losers. If the pool partially redistributes, incumbent DGR charities competing in the same donor markets lose share. Public data can't model actual losses, so we do a descriptive exposure analysis: who competes (by purpose proxy and size band), how donation-dependent they are, how many are highly exposed (>50% donation dependence).
  7. Validate everything against published anchors. Every build cross-checks totals against ATO publications and ACNC lodgment counts — 22 automated pass/fail assertions — so figures quoted in campaign material can be defended.

Results so far

  • Layer 1 scenario ranges (annual pool access, $m/yr, min–max across three allocation bases — count, donations, and revenue share): disaster preparedness 0.9–3.7, human rights 1.5–7.1, injury prevention 0.2–2.2, neighbourhood houses 0.3–6.2.
  • Incumbent exposure: e.g. neighbourhood houses' donor market has 6,552 DGR incumbents, 2,414 highly exposed; disaster preparedness only 354/64.
  • Layer 2 handoff dataset (dgr_gap_analysis, 198,141 charity-years): financials + DGR status + subtype + strata variables, ancillary funds excluded — ready for the teammate who owns the donations-gap modelling.
  • A finding along the way: DGR item numbers (Item 1 vs Item 2) don't exist in any bulk ABR product — documented in docs/abr_dgr_item_findings.md, with a provisional name-match ancillary flag (294 funds) as the workaround.

Steps for tackling my side

  1. Sync outputs to SharePoint — sync the hackathon Data library locally via OneDrive, set UNLOCKDGR_SHAREPOINT_PATH, run scripts/sync_outputs.R (now ships Parquet + CSV into latest/ and a dated versions/ snapshot).
  2. Onboard volunteers — point them at volunteer_setup.md and the data dictionary; they query via DuckDB, no repo access needed.
  3. Hand off Layer 2 — give the teammate dgr_gap_analysis.parquet, walk through strata definitions, leakage stats, and the "selection not causation" caveat; review their method.
  4. Review outstanding cohort candidates — the non-whitelisted rules (surf life saving, SAR, fire brigades, public safety) sit in analysis/subtype_candidates/; whitelist or reject, and add any manual ABNs to data/mappings/target_subtypes.csv.
  5. Write the narrative — turn Layer 1 ranges + leakage + incumbent exposure into campaign-facing findings, leading with neighbourhood houses (lowest leakage, most to gain) and honest scenario framing.
  6. Combine layers — once Layer 2 lands, present the two layers side by side (never summed without caveats) as the total reform benefit picture.
  7. Refresh before final submission — re-run tar_make, confirm all 22 validation checks pass, re-sync SharePoint, commit.
