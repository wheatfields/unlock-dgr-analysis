1. The spiel (~2 minutes, follows the 4 charts)

Setup — the pool (30s)

"Ancillary funds — PAFs and PuAFs — are Australia's tax-advantaged giving vehicles. Money goes in as deductible donations and must come out at 4–5% of assets a year, but only to DGR-endorsed charities. That outflow is now $1.5 billion a year — $914m from private funds, $613m from public — sitting on top of $17.5 billion in net assets, and it's grown roughly tenfold since the PAF regime began. DGR reform doesn't just change donor deductions; it changes who is allowed to receive this pool."

The fence (20s)

"Today only ~20k of the ~50k charities with current financials hold DGR — 40%. But that 40% holds 78% of donations and 62% of revenue. So the 60% locked out are overwhelmingly the smaller, less-fundable charities — exactly the ones a distribution pool could matter most for."

The scenarios (40s)

"We can't observe who actually receives the pool — recipient-level flows are never published. So we bound it: four eligibility scenarios — status quo, the PC's redesign, our expansion model, and universal DGR — crossed with three mechanical sharing rules: proportional to donations, to revenue, or equal per charity. Under the expansion model, ~13,000 newly eligible charities could access $109m–$600m a year of the pool. Donations-weighted is the plausible floor; count is an access ceiling, not a forecast."

The punchline (30s)

"The PC's redesign and our expansion let in the same gainers. The difference is the ~2,700 charities that lose DGR under the PC's pure change — that displaces $150–210m a year of their current pool access. Expansion displaces zero, and it costs the gainers only $10–50m a year of access. In other words: expansion buys 'nobody loses' almost for free. That's the key message."

One-liner: "There's a $1.5bn/yr pool only 40% of charities can touch; expansion opens it to ~13,000 more charities for $109–600m/yr of access, and unlike the PC's version, defunds nobody."

2. Deep dive — "How do you know who receives the pool today?"

Headline answer: We don't — and neither does anyone outside the ATO. The recipient-level data exists, but it is collected as protected taxpayer information and has never been published. That is precisely why this analysis models access under transparent rules rather than claiming to predict flows. Say this proactively — it converts the weakest-looking point into the strongest.

  1. The government collects it — recipient by recipient. The ATO's Ancillary Fund Return (NAT 73640), Section D "Distributions made", requires every non-charity fund to list the name, ABN and dollar amount for each recipient of every distribution. Funds registered as charities (most PAFs) lodge equivalent detail via the ACNC Annual Information Statement's ancillary-fund questions. So this is not a collection gap — it is a publication gap.
  2. Why it never becomes public: taxpayer confidentiality. Return data is protected information under the tax secrecy provisions (Taxation Administration Act 1953, Sch 1 Div 355). The ATO can only release it as aggregates. What it publishes — annual Taxation Statistics ancillary fund tables (our Tables 3 & 4) — stops at fund-type level: counts of funds, total donations in, total distributions out, net assets, split PAF vs PuAF. No recipient identity, no sector split, nothing below the aggregate.
  3. The ACNC side doesn't fill the gap either. The published AIS dataset does not include grant-recipient detail, and private ancillary funds can have their details withheld from the public register (we observe ~580 withheld ABNs in the data). Some funds itemise grants in PDF financial statements on the register, but small funds have reduced reporting obligations and the coverage is partial and unstructured — there is no comprehensive, machine-readable source.
  4. The recipient side is blind too. Charities report "donations and bequests" as a single aggregate line in the AIS. An ancillary-fund grant is indistinguishable from an ordinary donation in the recipient's return — so you cannot reconstruct the flows from the receiving end either. (We exploit this the other way: total reported donations acts as a hard ceiling on what any group could have absorbed.)
  5. International contrast. In the US, private foundations' Form 990-PF — including the full grants list — is public by law, which is why US grant-flow research is possible. Australia has no equivalent disclosure regime for ancillary funds.
  6. The PC hit the same wall. The PC's Future Foundations for Giving (2024) repeatedly flagged the weak public evidence base on philanthropy and recommended improved public reporting on ancillary ("giving") funds and giving generally. If the PC — with information-gathering powers — treated this as a data gap to be fixed, it is entirely defensible that our approach is bounding scenarios, not flow prediction. (Verify the exact recommendation number in the final report before quoting it on stage.)
  7. Therefore: the method is the message. Given the gap, the honest approach is mechanical allocation rules (donations / revenue / count) that bound the answer, validated against the hard donations ceiling. Anyone claiming to know the actual distribution of the pool is claiming access to data that does not publicly exist.

Soundbite: "The ATO collects every grant, name and ABN — Section D of the ancillary fund return — but tax secrecy means it only ever publishes the total. We're modelling access precisely because the flow data is locked in the ATO. If the government published it, we'd happily replace our bounds with the real numbers — and the PC recommended exactly that kind of transparency."
