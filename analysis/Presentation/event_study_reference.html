1. The spiel (~2.5 minutes, follows the charts)

Setup (20s)

"The whole DGR reform debate hangs on one empirical question: does endorsement actually move donations? We answered it from the charity side: 646 established charities that gained DGR between FY2019 and FY2024 — ACNC annual returns joined to ABR endorsement dates, with start-ups and ancillary funds excluded so we're watching real organisations before and after the switch."

The headline — growth ladder (25s)

"Line every charity up from worst donation change to best, in CPI-adjusted dollars. The median charity grew +30% in real terms — +47% nominal. It's not universal: about 1 in 3 fell, which is exactly why we quote the median, not a mean inflated by a few charities that went from nothing to something."

The timing — staggered cohorts (30s)

"Here's what points to DGR as the impact. Each endorsement cohort, indexed to its own pre-endorsement level, lifts off in its own endorsement year — not in any common calendar year. If this were COVID, inflation, or an economy-wide giving trend, all four panels would rise together in the same year. They don't."

Where it lands (30s)

"The growth is concentrated in small charities — those raising under $10k a year before endorsement — while established six-figure fundraisers barely move. We re-cut it by the ACNC's own revenue-based size categories, classified strictly on pre-endorsement data, and got the same picture. Why that matters: the ~17,900 charities the reform would newly endorse have median donations of ~$4,700 a year — squarely in the band with the strongest observed effect."

Robustness (20s)

"Could charities be ramping up before endorsement — applying with a campaign lined up? Two checks say no: there's no pre-trend (median drift from year −2 to −1 is ~0%, about half rose), and restricting the baseline to two-plus years before endorsement moves the estimate by ~0.3 points. The +30% is robust."

The punchline — the elasticity dial (35s)

"Everything in this debate is a position on one dial: the price elasticity of giving. Deductibility cuts the price of a $1 gift to $0.68. Our +30% implies an elasticity of about −0.67 — inside the PC's own predicted range of −0.48 to −1.67. The PC measured the dial from the donor side and said the charity-side approach was 'not feasible, given insufficient data'. This is that measurement — and it independently lands where their donor-side estimate said it should."

One-liner: "646 charities that gained DGR grew donations ~30% in real terms, timed to their own endorsement year, concentrated in exactly the small-charity profile the reform would endorse — and the implied elasticity sits inside the PC's own range."

2. Deep dive — "How do you know DGR caused this?"

Headline answer: We never claim a randomised experiment — the notebook itself says other variables can contribute. What we claim is an identification stack: five independent checks that all point the same way, plus external validation against the PC's donor-side estimate. Say the limits proactively; they're the reason the claims are framed as "points to" rather than "proves".

  1. Within-charity comparison. Every charity is compared to itself — post-endorsement mean vs its own pre-endorsement mean. Time-invariant differences between charities (cause, location, size, competence) cancel out.
  2. Staggered timing is the killer chart. Four cohorts endorsed in four different years each lift off at their own endorsement year. Any confounder would need to hit FY2019 switchers in 2019, FY2020 switchers in 2020, and so on — while leaving each cohort's pre-period flat. COVID, JobKeeper, inflation and giving trends are all calendar-year shocks; they cannot produce this pattern.
  3. Inflation is handled, not hand-waved. All dollars deflated with ABS CPI (6401.0, FY averages, FY2019 = 100). The +47% nominal becomes +30% real — we lead with the real number.
  4. No anticipation. Median within-charity drift from event year −2 to −1 is ~0% (about half rose) — charities were not already ramping up when they applied. A baseline restricted to years ≤ −2 shifts the median by ~0.3 points.
  5. The effect appears where the mechanism predicts. DGR unlocks deductible giving and foundation money — a channel that matters for a charity raising $2k/yr and is rounding error for one raising $2m. That's exactly the gradient observed, in both the donations-band split and the outcome-safe ACNC revenue-size split (classified only on pre-endorsement years).
  6. External validation. The implied elasticity (≈ −0.67) sits inside the PC's donor-side range (Finding 4.1: −0.48 to −1.67, ALife panel of 1.5m taxpayers). Two completely different methods, two different datasets, one consistent answer.

Honest limits (volunteer these):

  1. Selection into applying. Charities that seek DGR may be growth-oriented. The no-pre-trend check bounds this (they weren't already growing), but motivation coinciding with endorsement can't be fully excluded. There is no control group of identical charities denied DGR.
  2. Bundled channels. The uplift includes individual deductibility and newly unlocked foundation/ancillary money — the AIS reports one donations line. This is why you must never add pool-access dollars on top of the +30%: it double-counts.
  3. The PC's caveat cuts both ways. The PC said the charity-side approach was "not feasible, given insufficient data" (Technical Paper, p. 421). Partly right — which is why this is a plausibility check that happens to land inside their range, not a replacement elasticity estimate.

Soundbite: "Five checks, one answer: charities compared to themselves, lift-off timed to each cohort's own endorsement year, inflation stripped out, no pre-endorsement ramp-up, and the effect concentrated exactly where the mechanism says it should be. And the implied elasticity lands inside the PC's own donor-side range — measured from the opposite end of the transaction."

3. The elasticity dial, unpacked

One equation carries the whole argument: uplift = (1/0.68)|ε| − 1. Each moving part, in order:

  1. The tax rate t sets the price cut. A donor gives $1 to a DGR charity, claims the deduction, gets t back at tax time. Net cost = 1 − t. At t = 32% (≈ donation-weighted average marginal rate), the price of giving $1 is $0.68. That price cut is the only thing DGR endorsement changes in the model.
  2. Elasticity ε is the behavioural dial. % change in giving per % change in price. Negative: price falls, giving rises. It is the unknown the whole policy debate is fighting over — every position in the debate is a value of ε.
  3. The log-log model converts the price cut into a predicted uplift. Constant elasticity means giving G = A·pε, so when price moves from $1.00 to $0.68: G₁/G₀ = (0.68)ε = (1/0.68)|ε|. Why a power, not "32% × ε"? Elasticity is a local percentage-on-percentage concept; a 32% price cut is a large discrete jump, so the local response has to be compounded across the whole cut — that's the power form. The linear shortcut overstates at this size.
  4. Turning the dial reproduces every position in the debate:
    εPredicted upliftWho says this
    −0.48+20%PC range, bottom (Finding 4.1)
    −0.67+30%← our event study, real (inverted from observed uplift)
    −1.00+47%Treasury-efficiency threshold (PC Box B.1) — equals our nominal median exactly
    −1.50+78%What the "$1 → $1.50" claim requires
    −1.67+90%PC range, top
  5. Inverting: from measured uplift back to the dial. The PC went dial → uplift (estimate ε from donor tax data, predict giving). We went the other way: |ε| = ln(1 + uplift) / ln(1/0.68) = ln(1.30) / 0.386 ≈ 0.68. Same dial, measured from the opposite end of the transaction — and it lands inside their range.
  6. The "$1 → $1.50" claim is a different reading of the same dial — and the uplift is a direct test of it. Treasury efficiency asks: how much new giving does $1 of forgone revenue buy? With constant elasticity, new giving per tax-$ = (1 − (1−t)|ε|) / t — which equals exactly $1.00 at |ε| = 1 for any tax rate (the break-even, PC Box B.1), and ≈ |ε| for the local approximation advocacy pieces use. The bridge to our result runs in three steps:
    (a) measured +30% uplift ⇒ |ε| ≈ 0.67;
    (b) |ε| = 0.67 at t = 32% ⇒ ≈ $0.71 of new giving per tax dollar;
    (c) the $1.50 claim needs |ε| ≈ 1.5, which predicts a +78% real uplift — the 646 switchers should have grown 2.5× more than they did. So the event study is not just context for the "$1 → $1.50" claim: it is an empirical test, and the claim fails it. The honest line: the evidence supports ~70c of new giving per tax dollar — the conservative end of the PC range, defensible on other grounds, but not self-financing.
  7. Sensitivities and assumptions to know: (a) the curve is anchored on t — at t = 45% (top marginal rate) the price is $0.55 and the same ε ≈ −0.67 predicts ~+49%, so the 32% average-rate choice is load-bearing; (b) constant elasticity across a 32% price jump is an assumption, not a fact; (c) the model assumes donors claim the deduction (non-itemisers dilute the effective price cut); (d) our measured uplift bundles the pure price response with new-donor and foundation/ancillary access — so the implied −0.67 is best read as an upper bound on the price elasticity alone, which strengthens, not weakens, the "conservative end of the range" framing.

Soundbite: "Deductibility cuts the price of a $1 gift to 68 cents. How much giving that buys depends on one number — the price elasticity. The PC estimated it from 1.5 million donors' tax returns; we measured it from the charities' side; both land in the same place, around two-thirds. That's real uplift, but it also means the '$1 buys $1.50' claim — which needs an elasticity of 1.5 — isn't what the evidence shows."

How to read the dial chart (chart 5)

The axes. The x-axis is the disputed number: the price elasticity of giving |ε| — how strongly donors respond when the price of giving falls. The y-axis is what that response would look like in the data: the predicted donation uplift from gaining DGR. The black curve is the conversion between them (uplift = (1/0.68)|ε| − 1). Every position in the policy debate is a point on this one curve.

The two halves of the x-axis. ε = −1 splits the chart: everything left of −1 means each $1 of forgone tax revenue buys less than $1 of new giving (a subsidy with leakage); everything right of −1 means the subsidy multiplies — more than $1 of new giving per tax dollar. The treasury reading is built into the axis itself: at the margin, new giving per tax dollar ≈ |ε| — so ε = −0.5 reads "$0.50", −1 reads "$1.00", −1.5 reads "$1.50". The "$1 → $1.50" claim is the point ε = −1.5.

The direction of inference. The PC went x → y: estimate ε from donor tax data, predict the uplift. We went y → x: we measured the uplift (+30%) from 646 charities and read the curve backwards to find the ε it implies (−0.67). That's why our dot is evidence, not assumption.

The three marked points. Teal = our measurement (+30% → −0.67, reading ~$0.70 per tax dollar). Gold = break-even (−1 → +47% — which happens to equal our nominal median exactly). Red = the "$1 → $1.50" claim (−1.5 → +78% — an uplift 2.5× what we observed). The green band is the PC's estimated range (−0.48 to −1.67).

One-sentence read: "Our measured dot lands inside the PC's band, on the conservative side of break-even — and a long way short of the +78% the $1.50 claim would require."
